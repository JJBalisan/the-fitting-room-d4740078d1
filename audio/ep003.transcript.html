Learn: what fabric weight tells you

Trouser and shirt listings often quote a weight in grams per square metre. It is the mass of one square metre of finished cloth, and it is the quickest single number for judging what a garment will do.

Take two ISTO trousers. The brown chinos use a three hundred and forty gram organic cotton twill. The baggy chinos use a three hundred gram cloth. That forty gram gap is real. A heavier cloth packs more yarn into the same area, so it hangs with more structure, resists creasing at the knee, and takes longer to dry. A lighter twill drapes and moves more, and is cooler, but it shows wear sooner at the seat and the pockets.

What the number does not tell you is how that mass is built. Weight depends on three separate things: how thick the yarn is, how many threads there are per centimetre, and the weave. A three hundred and forty gram twill made of coarse, short yarn can feel harsher and pill sooner than a two hundred and eighty gram cloth made of long, combed fibre. Twill, where each weft thread passes over two or more warp threads in a diagonal pattern, can carry more threads than a plain weave, so at the same yarn size it is heavier and more supple.

The number also describes the cloth before you wash it. Cotton shrinks along the warp, and a stated weight is usually measured on the raw or finished fabric, not after your first hot wash.

There is a practical vegan angle. In wool, weight is partly a stand-in for warmth, because air trapped in the crimped fibre does the insulating. Cotton has almost no crimp, so its weight is mostly about structure, not warmth. If you are replacing a wool piece with cotton, a higher weight gets you the hang and the opacity, but not the insulation. For that, you add layers instead.

Try this. Pick up two pairs of trousers you own and check the label or the listing for the weight. Compare how each hangs from the hip. Then ask whether the difference comes from weight, or from cut and fibre.

Style: where a top ends against a high waistband

High-rise, wide trousers worn with a fluid top, or a boxy top cropped at the hip, raise one question. Where should the hem of the top meet the waistband? That spot sets where the body appears to divide, and there are three common answers: fully tucked, half-tucked with just the front in, or untucked.

Each one moves the visible waist. A full tuck with a belt puts the break at the waistband. With a high-rise trouser that sits above the natural waist, that lengthens the leg line. An untucked hem that ends at the hip makes a second horizontal line below the waistband. That shortens the leg visually, but it suits boxy, cropped tops, because the volume stays high and the trousers carry the length. A half-tuck is the compromise, and it breaks the line asymmetrically.

Taste is genuinely split here. One view says a tuck is the cleaner choice with high-rise trousers, since it shows the waistband and keeps proportions legible. The other says an untucked boxy top is the whole point of the silhouette, and tucking it spoils the volume you wanted. Neither is law. The convention exists to control where the eye lands, and it stops mattering when a top is cropped enough that the hem already clears the waistband.

This week's exercise. Take one top and one pair of high-rise trousers you own, and stand in front of a mirror. Photograph all three versions from the same distance. Then look at the photos, not the mirror. Note which version makes the leg look longest, and which makes the top look most intentional. They are often not the same one.

New in

First, the Yes Friends Oxford Shirt, thirty-five pounds. It is a one hundred per cent organic cotton oxford in a lightly textured weave, with a unisex relaxed fit, recycled plastic buttons, and a G O T S certified, solar-assisted factory. It comes in white, pine and Oxford blue. It is a plain, wearable base layer for colour pairing, for instance a pine shirt under a rust or mustard vest. Delivery is four pounds standard, free over a hundred pounds. When this was checked, the white was showing out of stock, so confirm your colour and size before you plan around it.

Second, the Vivobarefoot Primus Lite IV, a hundred pounds. This is a barefoot training shoe, so it is a practical, light trainer rather than a style piece. The upper is one hundred per cent recycled polyester. The lining is T P U, recycled polyester and polyurethane, the laces are recycled polyester, and the insole is recycled polyurethane. Note the brand's own wording. It marks its whole collection as vegan with an asterisk. Its explanation is that the main materials are synthetic, but it does not yet have transparency over small components such as adhesives and dyes, so it cannot guarantee that the shoes meet vegan best practice. Standard delivery is five pounds, free over eighty pounds.

Deals

Nothing time-limited cleared this week. The Watson and Wolfe up to eighty per cent off is an undated closing-down sale rather than a real deadline. The one bag from it that could be checked, the Maddox tote at a hundred and twenty-five pounds, down from two hundred and twenty-five, is out of stock.

One caveat applies to the whole sale. Buying from a brand that is winding down carries aftersales risk. Returns, repairs and the one-year guarantee all depend on the company still existing.

Worth the premium

The ISTO Chinos in Brown cost a hundred and forty-five euros. That is over the hundred pound line this show uses for clothing that is not footwear, and the premium looks justified for three reasons.

The cloth is a three hundred and forty gram, G O T S certified organic cotton twill. The buttons are corozo, which is a plant seed, not horn or shell. And ISTO publishes what each component of the price cost, including nine euros twenty-three for the fabric and twenty-three cents for the buttons.

UK orders ship duties paid, so there is no import charge at the door. The price is in euros, and the sterling figure is set at checkout. The cut is tapered, not wide, so treat it as a neat brown, rust-adjacent trouser rather than a wide high-rise one. It was listed as in stock on the twenty-ninth of October.

A brand to know: Neem London

Neem London is an independent London menswear brand. It makes shirts and trousers in Romania from Italian fabric developed with small mills.

Its distinctive idea is circularity. Its shirts are made from over fifty per cent recycled cotton blended with organic cotton. It runs a take-back scheme that recycles clothes from any brand and pays credit toward your next order. And it states a carbon figure per shirt: two point seven one kilograms of carbon dioxide equivalent, which it says is fifty-four per cent less than a standard cotton shirt. That figure is the brand's own claim. The range includes double-pleated, wider-leg trousers in organic cotton and recycled cotton twill, plus Tencel shirts, so there is draped, relaxed tailoring and soft colour.

Now the weaknesses. This is not a vegan brand. The same site sells Z Q merino wool, cashmere and recycled wool, and a recycled leather belt. So every item needs checking for fibre, and for buttons, before you buy. Prices are high: trousers sit at a hundred and sixty to a hundred and sixty-five pounds, shirts at a hundred and forty pounds, with a two-shirt deal at two hundred and thirty. Several items are pre-order. And its environmental figures are the brand's own; no third-party certificate is stated. This is a profile of the brand, not a recommendation of a specific piece, because no individual Neem garment has been checked for buttons or composition.

The verdict

The one thing to buy is the ISTO Chinos in Brown, if a tapered cut suits you. It is the only item this week that cleared every check: it is in stock, it ships duty paid, and the buttons are plant-based. It is not a wide high-rise trouser, and it would be wrong to pretend otherwise. If a hundred and forty-five euros is too much, the Yes Friends Oxford at thirty-five pounds is the cheaper, lower-risk buy, provided your colour is back in stock.

The bad buy is the ISTO baggy chinos, and it does not pass. Recycled horn buttons sound like a sustainability point, but horn is still an animal material. Check the button line on every ISTO trouser page.

The broader point is that the fibre and button detail could only be confirmed on a handful of items this week, and a lot of what looked promising failed on a detail, not on the headline fabric. Nothing else tracked this week is strong enough to put beside the chinos.

On ethics claims, Vivobarefoot's own asterisk is the honest version of a problem most brands do not disclose at all. Be warier of a bag listing that says vegan leather and quotes a recycled percentage but never names the material.
