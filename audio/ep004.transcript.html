Today: why a polyurethane coating fails, judging an outfit at three distances, and a brand worth knowing with some real reasons for care.

Learn: why a polyurethane coating fails

Most vegan rainwear, and most vegan leather, share one ingredient: polyurethane, usually shortened to P U. A P U jacket is a woven polyester cloth with a thin layer of polyurethane on one side. The cloth gives strength. The coating is what stops water. Rains describes its shell the same way: a polyester fabric with a P U finish and welded seams, rated for light rain.

The coating is a polymer built from two kinds of building block, and the weak point is the chemical link between them. In the older, cheaper kind of P U, that link is an ester, and esters react slowly with water. Humidity alone is enough, given time. The coating loses its elasticity, goes tacky or flakes, and the jacket starts to leak from the inside of the fabric outward. This is called hydrolysis, and it is why a P U jacket that sat unused in a cupboard can fall apart while a worn one survives.

Polyether-based P U resists water much better, but it costs more, and a retailer rarely says which one it used. Nothing on a typical product page tells you.

Seams are the second mechanism. Stitching puts thousands of needle holes through a waterproof layer. A welded seam fuses the fabric edges with heat and removes the holes, which is why it matters more than the headline waterproof number. It also means the jacket cannot be repaired the way a stitched one can.

Here is where this meets the vegan constraint. The animal-free option for waterproofing is almost always a petrochemical coating, so vegan and long-lived pull against each other. A waxed cotton jacket would last, but the wax is often beeswax or paraffin, and the paraffin versions are a separate question. The practical consequence is to treat a P U shell as a consumable with a life of some years. Wash it as the label says, because heat and detergent speed up the breakdown, and buy it for the weather you actually have.

Style: judging an outfit at three distances

One idea this week: an outfit is read at three distances, and it can pass at one and fail at another.

At arm's length you see texture, stitching, the quality of a fabric's surface. Across a room you see the outline and the large blocks of colour, and the finer detail disappears. From the far end of a street you see only the silhouette and the single strongest contrast. A lot of fashion advice quietly addresses only one of these. Photographs flatten everything to the middle distance, which is why an outfit that looks sharp in a mirror selfie can look shapeless in person from across a room, or the reverse.

The exercise: put on something you wear a lot. Look at it in the mirror at arm's length and write down the one thing you notice. Then walk to the far side of the room, or take a photo and shrink it on your phone until you can't read any detail, and write down the one thing you notice. If the two answers are the same, the outfit is consistent. If they differ, you have found which part of it is doing the work at which distance. Most people find that their trousers carry the far view and their top carries the near one.

There are two reasonable positions on what to do with that. One says the far view is the one that matters because it is how most people meet you, so design the silhouette first and let texture follow. The other says the near view is where you actually live in the clothes, so choose fabrics you like to touch and let the silhouette be a by-product. Neither is wrong. The convention that a strong silhouette beats a good fabric stops applying when you spend your day close to people, at a desk or a table, where the arm's-length view is the only one anyone gets.

A brand to know: Thought

Thought is a London label that began in the nineteen nineties as Braintree Clothing and rebranded in twenty seventeen. It built its name on bamboo viscose, G O T S certified organic cotton, hemp and Tencel lyocell, the fibres behind soft, draped tops.

The reasons to be careful are significant. It entered administration in September twenty twenty-three and was bought by Wourth Group, formerly WoolOvers, in twenty twenty-four. The product range was cut back and is now mostly socks and basics. Customer reviews on Trustpilot since then are poor, with repeated complaints about fabric thinning and holes, and about customer service, though those reviews are unverified opinion. Independent write-ups flag missing disclosure of final production sites and no clear living-wage evidence. And it is not a vegan brand: it also uses wool, so every item needs checking for fibre, and the cellulose fabrics are the ones that draw the most wear complaints.

No Thought garment is recommended this week. The brand is worth knowing as a source of cellulose basics and socks, with those quality caveats.

The verdict

There is no strong match in this week's clearances, so no standout pick. The one thing worth buying is practical: the Rains Jacket at eighty-nine pounds, only if you do not already own a shell, and knowing it is a light-rain jacket with a P U coat that will age. If you do own a shell, buy nothing.

The Storm Breaker is not a better buy than the plain Jacket. It costs ten pounds more, has a more technical look, and carries the same W three rating.

On Rains itself: the brand publishes only a self-written code of conduct and regulatory compliance, with no third-party certification or factory disclosure to be found, and it states no insulation fill on its insulated pieces. That is why only uninsulated shells appear here.

One more caution. I S T O's Baggy Chinos in charcoal list recycled horn buttons in their specification, so they fail for anyone avoiding animal materials. Last week's pick, the I S T O Chinos in Brown, was recorded as having corozo buttons, so that recommendation is unaffected, but the baggy cut in another colour needs checking.
