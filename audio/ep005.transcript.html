Why a cotton tee dyes unevenly

This week's pick is the Yes Friends cord trousers at forty-nine pounds, and the most time-sensitive item is the Vivobarefoot Black Friday sale, which runs from the tenth to the thirtieth of November. The cord trousers had only five pairs left when last checked, so they will not wait for Black Friday. Today we look at garment dyeing and why the colour of a cotton tee settles unevenly, at how texture holds a tonal outfit together, and at Colorful Standard, a Danish maker of organic cotton basics.

Learn: Garment dyeing and uneven colour

Most cotton clothing is dyed before it is a garment. The yarn, or the knitted fabric, goes into a dye bath, comes out one even colour, and is then cut and sewn. Garment dyeing reverses the order. The piece is cut and sewn from undyed fabric, and the finished garment goes into the machine. Colorful Standard, this week's brand to know, describes exactly this: its garments are sewn from undyed fabric first and dyed to order.

That mechanism explains both the look and the cost. A finished garment has thick and thin zones: double-layer collars, folded hems, seam allowances. Dye liquor reaches those zones at different rates, and the cotton takes up different amounts, so collars and seams come out a little darker or paler than the body. The brand calls this an authentic, lived-in character, and that is a fair description of what a buyer sees. It also means two garments in the same named colour will not match exactly. A tee and a crew bought separately in the same colour can differ.

The upside is on the supply side. Because undyed stock can be dyed after the orders arrive, a maker does not have to guess which colours will sell, and leftover fabric can go back into production, which the brand cites as a waste reduction. The garment is also pre-washed in the process, so it has already done most of its shrinking. What you pay for is that every batch is a small run in the dye house, rather than one large run of rolled fabric.

Thread is the detail that gives the game away. Cotton thread takes cotton dye. Polyester thread does not, which is why some garment-dyed clothes show pale stitching against a deep body colour. If you look at the seams of a garment-dyed piece and the stitching has taken the colour, the maker sewed it in cotton.

Now where this meets the vegan constraint. Most modern dyes are synthetic, but the category does have animal-derived members, such as carmine red, which comes from cochineal insects. Dye is also one of the things Vivobarefoot says it cannot yet fully vouch for, which is the source of the asterisk on its vegan label. A plain brand statement that a garment is vegan rarely says anything about the dye house.

Style: Texture contrast and the tonal outfit

Earlier topics dealt with light against dark, and with where trousers cut the body. This one is about surface. In a tonal outfit, one colour from head to toe, the eye has no colour change to follow, so it follows the surface: matte against sheen, flat against ribbed, smooth against pile. Cream on cream and white on white depend on this. A cream matte cotton shirt over cream satin trousers reads as two pieces. The same shirt over cream cotton trousers reads as a single flat shape.

Corduroy is a useful case to look at this week, because its pile catches light differently depending on direction and on the viewing angle. A single pair of cord trousers shifts between lighter and darker across one day without any change of colour.

There are two positions here. One says contrast in texture is what makes a tonal outfit look deliberate, and that an outfit in a single texture looks like a uniform or a set. The other says tonal dressing works best when the textures are close and the interest comes from the cut, and that big texture jumps look busy. Both are correct in some cases. The convention exists to stop a monochrome outfit reading as a flat block. It stops applying when the cut already supplies the interest, for example a wide leg with a long fall.

Try this. Pick the two garments you own that are closest in colour, put them on together, and photograph yourself in black and white. If the two pieces merge into one block, the textures are too alike for the effect you wanted. If they separate clearly, then try the same pair with one piece swapped for something in the same colour but a different surface, and compare the two photos.

A brand to know: Colorful Standard

Colorful Standard is a Danish brand that makes organic cotton basics in its own factory in Barcelos, Portugal, and sells them in a wide range of colours. The Classic Organic Tee comes in more than forty colourways. It is a Certified B Corporation, and says its Portuguese factory holds a G O T S scope certificate for organic cotton apparel. Its cotton is certified to the O C S one hundred standard and is sourced from India and Turkey. It owns the factory and keeps its suppliers within about fifty kilometres of it.

The interesting part is the colour range. Confident colour pairings, pink with green, orange with green, mustard with bottle green, depend on being able to find the exact shades. A single maker with garment-dyed tees, crews and sweatpants in dozens of colours, at one fit and weight, makes pairings easy to assemble. The Classic Organic Crew is sixty pounds in one hundred per cent organic cotton, regular fit, garment-dyed and made in Portugal, in colours that include Midnight Forest. Free delivery applies over sixty pounds, and returns are open for ninety days.

Its weaknesses are specific. It sells knitwear in recycled wool alongside the cotton, so a vegan shopper has to check item by item. The brand does not say whether UK customers pay import VAT or duty on top of the price, so check at checkout. It publishes no price breakdown of the kind ISTO does. And it makes basics: there is no drape, no print and no wide trouser, so it is a colour supplier, not a whole wardrobe. Its own B Corp page says the assessment showed it still has work to do, without saying where.

The verdict

The one thing to buy this week is the Yes Friends cord trousers at forty-nine pounds. It is the only item that cleared with a sterling price, a stated composition, and buttons that are not animal-derived. It is not a certain fit: the page gives no cut, so buy it knowing it may be a regular leg, and UK returns cost four pounds. Stock was down to five when last checked, so if you want them, do not wait for Black Friday. None of this week's cleared items is a perfect match for anyone's wardrobe, and I will not present it as one.

The weakest case among the things I could not recommend is Matt and Nat. Its sale prices look large, for instance the Sam crossbody at thirty-nine pounds against sixty-five. But the page names no material beyond vegan leather with fifty-eight per cent recycled content, it shows as sold out, and the colour on show is a pre-order. I would not buy it on that page.

Two brands' own evidence falls short of their claims. Sara's Threads' home page calls its fabric organic, while the tee's product page says only one hundred per cent cotton, and its sourcing page lists wholesale suppliers that sell both organic and conventional fabric, so organic is not shown for this tee. And Vivobarefoot's vegan asterisk remains a belief, by its own account, because of adhesives and dyes.

One more caution. Vivobarefoot has not published which styles are in the sale, and back in late September its sale page listed a leather sneaker beside the vegan styles. If you shop that sale for a vegan pair, cross-check each shoe against the vegan collection first.
