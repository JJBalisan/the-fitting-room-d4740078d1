How machine embroidery works

This week's pick is a fifty-three pound embroidered jumper from Sara's Threads, and the episode is about how machine embroidery works and why it suits some garments and ruins others. The most time-sensitive item is the closing-down sale at Watson and Wolfe, which is still taking orders with no published last-order date. It is a thin week for new products, so this is a shorter episode.

Learn: how machine embroidery works

A sewing machine makes a seam by interlocking two threads. An embroidery machine uses the same stitch, but a computer moves the fabric under the needle so the stitches build an image instead of joining two pieces.

The design is a file in which a person, called a digitiser, has decided the stitch type, direction, length and density for every area. Three stitch types do most of the work. A running stitch draws a line. A satin stitch is a tight zigzag that makes a narrow raised column, used for outlines and lettering. A fill stitch, often called tatami, lays rows of short stitches to cover an area.

Density is the part that decides whether a garment survives. Every stitch is a needle hole, and every thread pulls the fabric inward. Pack a large area densely onto a light cotton jersey and the cloth puckers around the design, goes stiff, and eventually tears along the line of holes. Makers manage this with a stabiliser, a backing sheet that holds the fabric flat while it is stitched, and by spacing the stitches out. This is why embroidery sits better on a heavy, stable fabric, such as a mid-weight fleece-back jumper, than on a thin tee. It is also why small, open designs wear better than a solid block of colour on a chest.

Embroidery also changes how the garment is cared for. The stitched area does not stretch the way the knit around it does, so a tee that shrinks or relaxes in the wash will distort the surroundings of the motif. Turning the garment inside out for washing protects the thread face. The backing on the inside can scratch, which is why some makers add a soft cover patch.

The vegan angle is the thread and the backing. Most embroidery thread is polyester or rayon, which is fine. Cotton and metallic threads exist too. Silk thread exists as a premium option, and some stabilisers and patches use wool felt. So a vegan label on the garment does not by itself say anything about what is stitched onto it. A maker who embroiders onto a bought-in blank, as the brand we will meet shortly does, controls the thread but not the fabric.

Style: scale of a print against your own frame

One idea this week. A graphic or motif has a scale, and that scale is read against your body rather than against the garment. A palm-sized motif on a broad chest reads as a small detail. The same motif on a narrow frame reads as a statement. Nothing about the design changed; the ratio did.

Try this. Take a tee or jumper you own that carries a print or embroidery. Stand in front of a mirror and note how wide the motif is compared with the width of your chest at the same height: roughly a quarter, a third or half. Then do the same with a plain garment where you judge the proportion right, and compare the two. Most people find their preference sits in a narrow band, and knowing it makes shopping faster, because product photos on a model of a different build mislead on exactly this ratio.

The conventions on this are contested. One view holds that a motif should be centred on the chest, or sit just left of centre, because that is where the eye lands. Another holds that off-centre, low or oversized placement is more interesting precisely because it is awkward, and streetwear leans on it. The first view is about looking composed. The second is about looking considered but unconventional. Neither is right; they serve different aims, and the mirror comparison tells you which one you are actually after.

A brand to know: Sara's Threads

Sara's Threads is a one-person, queer-owned London brand that machine-embroiders slogans and illustrations onto tees, jumpers, hats, socks and tote bags. Tees are twenty-eight or thirty-two pounds and jumpers fifty-three. It also runs a Pride collection, called Queen and Kings, and has a shop on Brick Lane that takes custom embroidery orders. The size range is inclusive, up to triple X L on the jumper.

What is good about it: the work is hers, the prices are low for embroidered garments, returns are thirty days on unworn items, and it is one of the few brands where you can see the maker. The Shroom jumper's page states its fibres.

The weaknesses are real. The garments are wholesale blanks, which the brand says come from A S Colour and S F Clothing. So what you are buying is a stranger's fabric with her embroidery on it, and the ethics of that fabric are not hers to vouch for. She makes everything herself, so dispatch takes about a week. The organic fabric claim on the site is not supported on the one product page that could be read, which states cotton and recycled polyester without the word organic. The Queen and Kings pieces are limited editions in small runs, so availability is thin.

The verdict

The one thing to buy is the Sara's Threads Shroom jumper at fifty-three pounds. It is not exciting and it is not new, but it is the only item this week whose own page states a fibre blend with no animal content, and there is no deadline pushing you.

Nothing that passed the checks looks like a bad buy. The Watson and Wolfe wallet at thirty-nine pounds is a fair price for the material, but it is not a pick, because the brand's ethics claims rest on its own word and the brand is closing.

One last point. Black Friday means nearly every brand is advertising discounts with no end dates. That says nothing about the clothes, but it means this was a quieter week for confirmed finds than usual.
